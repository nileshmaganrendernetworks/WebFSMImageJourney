DicePod — a $499 consumer vegetable dicer/slicer: is it possible, and what does using it look like?

Short answer: yes, buildable at $499 AUD RRP — every mechanism involved already exists in consumer and commercial products at this price. The visual sequence below shows the full user journey: potato in → 12 mm dice (no touching blades) → turn a dial → carrot in → 4 mm slices (still no blade change) → lift out one cartridge stack → dishwasher.

One honest caveat: of all the requirements, the two hardest are user-selectable dice sizes (interchangeable grids are easy; motorised size switching is harder) and zero manual blade changes across all cut types. Both are solved below with a carousel of grid cartridges + an internal, motorised blade/gate carousel so the user only turns a sealed external dial.

Jump:
Act I — Dice a potato at one size (no blade contact)

1Load the potato

t = 0 s · hands nowhere near blades

DicePod sits on the counter (30×24×36 cm — smaller than a Thermomix). Lid flips open, a whole medium potato drops into the 110 mm chute. The dial is already on “Dice 12 mm” from last time.

2Lid closed — press the button

t = 2 s · one touch, ~5 s cycle

Interlocked lid enables the motor. A 300 W gearmotor drives the plunger down while a thin disc blade slices the potato into slabs; the same stroke presses slabs through the 12 mm grid. An ejector comb sweeps between grid bars on every return stroke — nothing lodges in the grid.

3Dice lands in the catch bin

t ≈ 8 s total — vs ~60–120 s by knife

Cubes are discharged downward at speed (no horizontal ledge for food to rest on), and the comb's return stroke wipes the grid clean. The user lifts the bin: a full potato diced at 12 mm. They have touched produce twice — in and out — and blades zero times.

Act II — Slice a carrot, without the user changing any blade

4Turn the sealed dial: Dice → Slice

t = 10 s · the only “blade change” is a dial turn

The external dial drives a motorised carousel holding the grids and the slice gate. It rotates the 12 mm grid out and a plain gate in — the spinning disc now cuts plain 4 mm slices with nothing cross-cutting them. The user's hand never opens the lid or touches steel. (Dice size works the same way: dial selects 8 / 12 / 20 mm grid cartridges.)

5Carrot in — sliced in seconds

t ≈ 14 s total — vs ~30–90 s by knife

Same chute, same button. The disc spins, coins eject into the same bin on top of the potato dice (or a fresh bin). Total elapsed time for two different cut types on two vegetables: ~15 seconds, one dial turn, zero blade handling.

6Close-up: nothing to pick off

The “no cleaning food off blades” requirement

Anti-stick is engineered, not wiped: pieces exit at speed, the comb sweeps the grid every cycle, and all cut surfaces are electropolished stainless with no horizontal resting faces. Worst case with a starchy potato: a film that rinses off — handled by the dishwasher, not fingers.

Act III — From dirty to dishwasher in 10–15 seconds

7Unlatch and lift the wash stack

t ≈ 10 s — inside the 10–15 s budget

One thumb latch releases the whole food path as a nested stack: pusher, chute, blade cartridge, grid cartridge, catch bin. No tools, no unscrewing, no separating twenty small pieces — the five parts nest inside each other as they come off.

8Straight into the dishwasher

t ≈ 15 s total from “done cooking” to “machine washing”

The five parts drop onto the top rack — all stainless or dishwasher-safe polymer, no hidden crevices, seals or fasteners. The motor base never gets dirty: it's outside the food path and just gets a wipe. Sequence complete: potato diced, carrot sliced, machine washing — in about the time it takes to wash a knife and board.

Feasibility verdict & the honest risks

  • YES — $499 AUD Every core mechanism is proven at or below this price: grid+disc dicing (Breville Paradice 9 at $479–529, KitchenAid dicing kit at ~$450–500), ejector combs (commercial grid dicers), centrifugal discharge (Sammic/Urschel), tool-free dishwasher-safe stacks (Thermomix-style).
  • Main risk The internal blade/grid carousel (requirement: user never changes blades) is the novel part — it adds a small servo, position sensor and sealing effort, and is the main cost and reliability risk. No current consumer product does motorised cut-type switching; closest is manual grid swaps on the Paradice/KitchenAid.
  • De-scope option A V1 with dial-switched slice + one dice size (carriage moves the grid in/out of the path) covers the shown sequence exactly; the 3-size grid carousel can follow in V2 once V1 validates the market.
  • Watch-item Very soft/wet produce (ripe tomato) is the known enemy of grid dicing — spec the device for firm produce, as all grid dicers do.
